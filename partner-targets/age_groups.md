The simplest evidence-based split is:

| Age group                               | Strongest defensible claim                                                      | Best intervention framing                                                                       |

### 1. Adolescents and late teens: strongest target for augmentation

This is where the strongest causal-ish education evidence sits. Ritchie and Tucker-Drob’s meta-analysis found that additional education is associated with around **1–5 IQ points per additional year of education** across 42 datasets and more than 600,000 participants. Brinch and Galloway’s Norwegian compulsory-schooling study found that extra schooling in the middle teenage years had a sizeable effect on IQ measured at age 19. ([PubMed][1])

So, for IQMindware, **15–18** is probably the strongest “lifelong augmentation” audience. The claim should not be “guaranteed IQ increase”, but it is reasonable to say that adolescence and late schooling are periods where structured cognitive challenge, reasoning practice and education may have unusually durable effects.

### 2. University-age adults: promising, but not clean pre/post IQ evidence

The Clouston et al. birth-cohort study found that university completion was associated with higher adult fluid cognition after controlling for adolescent cognition, with an estimated offset of about **0.5–1.5 SD** lower adolescent cognition. However, this was not a direct pre/post-college IQ-gain study. It was observational cohort modelling: adolescent cognition, later educational attainment, and adult fluid cognition. ([OUP Academic][2])

Kremen et al. are the caution here. In their VETSA sample, after accounting for age-20 general cognitive ability, later education, occupational complexity and cognitive-intellectual activity explained little variance in late-midlife cognition. They explicitly suggest that education-related intellectual-capacity gains may plateau by late adolescence or early adulthood, with reverse causation playing a major role after that point. 

So, **18–24** is still a very attractive intervention group, but I would frame it as:

> a high-opportunity learning window where reasoning habits, problem-space control, study strategy and cognitive-control routines can be shaped during intensive education.

Not:

> college reliably raises IQ by 10–20 points.

### 3. Young adults after college: target performance and transfer, not “raise g” as the primary promise

For post-college adults, the evidence for large IQ-like gains from more education or cognitive activity becomes much weaker. Kremen et al. found that among men with the same education at age 20, later university education was associated with only about a **1.23 IQ-point** advantage at age 62 once age-20 GCA was considered. 

This does not mean young adults are poor targets. It means the product promise should shift from “raise intelligence permanently” to something like:

> improve cognitive performance under demand: attention control, working-memory control, reasoning strategy, learning efficiency and transfer across problem types.

That is probably where Trident-G is strongest commercially: **train the machinery of applied intelligence**, while measuring pre/post performance honestly.

### 4. Midlife adults: skill growth is plausible, especially when skills are used

The newer adult-skills literature is more optimistic than old cross-sectional ageing curves. A 2025 *Science Advances* study using German PIAAC-L longitudinal data found that literacy and numeracy skills can increase into the forties before later decline, and that decline at older ages was concentrated among people with below-average skill use. ([PMC][3])

This suggests **35–55** is a good market for professional cognitive performance. The right claim is not broad fluid-IQ increase. It is more like:

> cognitively demanding work and structured learning can maintain or improve adult skills, especially skills that are repeatedly used, updated and challenged.

For IQMindware, this age band could be framed around professional reasoning, decision quality, complex problem solving, and “keeping cognitive bandwidth high”.

### 5. Older adults: strongest claims are maintenance, reserve and selective improvement

For older adults, later-life university-style education does show interesting effects, but they are selective. The Tasmanian Healthy Brain Project found that further education in older adults improved cognitive reserve and selectively improved some cognitive functions, especially language-related capacity, rather than producing broad gains across working memory, executive function and episodic memory. ([PubMed][4])

The ACTIVE trial is also important: older adults trained in memory, reasoning or speed improved most clearly on the trained cognitive ability, with reasoning and speed benefits lasting up to 10 years, and reasoning training associated with less functional decline in self-reported instrumental activities of daily living. ([PMC][5])

So for **55–75**, the best framing is:

> maintain fluid performance, strengthen selected skills, preserve everyday cognitive function, and build reserve through sustained, demanding cognitive engagement.

Not:

> raise IQ broadly.

### 6. Cognitive training caveat: transfer is the hard part

The evidence on cognitive training is mixed. Working-memory training meta-analyses are especially cautionary: Melby-Lervåg, Redick and Hulme concluded that WM training improves trained or closely related WM tasks, but does not provide strong evidence for broad far transfer to intelligence or real-world cognition. ([PMC][6])

That actually supports your Trident-G positioning. The product should not be “n-back raises IQ”. It should be:

> adaptive cognitive training plus relational abstraction, strategy instruction, wrapper variation, delayed re-checks and transfer probes.

In other words, train and measure the transfer process itself, rather than assuming transfer from local task improvement.

### Practical IQMindware targeting hierarchy

For **maximum cognitive augmentation/lifelong impact**, target:

> **15–24**, especially 15–18 and early university-age learners.

For **professional performance and applied intelligence**, target:

> **25–55**, especially people in cognitively demanding roles who need better reasoning, decision-making and learning speed.

For **maintenance and cognitive longevity**, target:

> **55–75**, with claims around reserve, selective improvement, reasoning/speed maintenance and everyday functioning.

For **later-life support**, target:

> **75+**, but emphasise confidence, routines, mental activity, functional independence and compensatory strategies rather than IQ increase.

The cleanest scientific positioning is: **younger users are the best “augmentation” market; midlife users are the best “performance optimisation” market; older users are the best “maintenance and reserve” market.**

## Which Trident-G components should gain most?

I’d expect the strongest near-to-medium gains in this order:
